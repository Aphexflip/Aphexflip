# Why I Build

*Erik Erling | Engineer, maker, father, founder of RSYMO*

## I came to software from machines that had to work.

For most of my working life, I've been the person called when complicated equipment wasn't doing what it was supposed to do.

I've worked on industrial printing and cutting systems, metal 3D printers, and large electromagnets. I've installed machines, diagnosed failures, helped customers get back to work, and trained people to operate and understand the equipment in front of them.

One lesson stayed the same: a machine doesn't care how convincing your explanation sounds. It works or it doesn't. You follow the evidence, learn the system, find the actual constraint, and keep going until you understand the problem.

That is the mindset I brought into software.

## I can't stop asking what else is possible.

What fascinates me about AI isn't just that it can answer questions or produce code. It's that one curious person can now attempt things that used to require a much bigger team.

I keep coming back to the same questions.

Why can't my computers understand what I'm trying to accomplish across all my projects? Why should I have to keep explaining the same goals? Why can't an AI system organize the work, use the tools available to it, notice when something failed, and show me what actually happened?

Why can't we build technology that gives ordinary people extraordinary capabilities?

Those questions are why I started **RSYMO**, my independent software project, and **Taskmaster**, the private orchestration system behind much of my experimentation.

I'm working toward a system that can remember goals, coordinate tools and AI workers, make progress within defined permissions, preserve context, and verify results. Some of those pieces exist as code and tests today. Other pieces are still experiments. I don't want to confuse an ambitious design with a finished product.

## I like the ideas that sound a little ridiculous.

I might spend one day thinking about how AI characters could form their own goals, remember experiences, and change a simulated world instead of following a fixed script.

Another day, I'm exploring whether a society's standard of living can be modeled using actual housing, labor, and resource constraints.

Then it might be a multiplayer game, a dashboard that makes complex information understandable, or an experiment involving electricity, motion, and physical machinery.

That may look like a strange collection of projects. To me, it is the same question in different forms:

**How does this system really work, what is stopping it from doing more, and what could happen if we change that?**

## My approach is to build, test, and keep learning.

I didn't arrive in software through a conventional software engineering career. I came from years of solving real problems in the physical world, then started teaching myself new tools because I wanted to build more ambitious things.

I use AI coding assistants extensively. They help me research, implement, refactor, and test. I define what I want to accomplish, choose the constraints, review what comes back, investigate failures, and keep improving the result. I won't claim to have manually written every line.

I also won't call something reliable just because it generated successfully.

In industrial engineering, an untested fix is not the same as a repaired machine. I want the same discipline in software: clear behavior, evidence, honest limitations, and the willingness to find out what broke.

I'm still learning. That's part of what excites me. I want to work around people who know things I don't, challenge my assumptions, and care about building something that holds up outside a demo.

## The part that matters most to me

I'm a father. My kids are a big reason I want to build something lasting and worthwhile.

I want them to see that curiosity matters, that learning doesn't have to stop when you're an adult, and that you don't have to wait for someone else to give you permission to make something.

I want to make tools that give other people more time, more freedom, and more ability to turn their own ideas into reality.

**I don't want AI that only sounds impressive. I want to build systems that can do useful things, show their work, admit when they fail, and leave people in control.**

That's what I'm about, and that's the kind of work I want to spend my life doing.
